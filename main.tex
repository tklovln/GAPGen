\begin{abstract}
Generative image models make visual material for educational games cheap to produce, yet an image that looks right can still teach the wrong concept: a picture of one fourth may be cut into unequal parts. Common evaluators score aesthetics or text-image similarity, and vision-language model judges return holistic verdicts without checkable evidence. We present EduAssetVerify, a framework that represents each learning objective as formal educational constraints, asks a vision-language model only for visual evidence, measures geometry deterministically, and lets rules decide each constraint, answering Uncertain when the evidence is insufficient. To evaluate verifiers without human raters, we build a counterfactual benchmark of fraction, counting, and spatial assets in which each correct asset is paired with a minimally altered violation, and every label comes from a procedural renderer cross-checked by independent pixel measurement. Across 900 test images, two model families, and three passes, EduAssetVerify accepted none of the violated assets, whereas direct, chain-of-thought, and structured judges accepted 0.6 to 4.3 percent, at the price of sending 1.5 to 3.1 percent of assets to review and four to nine times the judges' cost. EduAssetVerify offers a reproducible way to check the instructional correctness of generated visual assets without human evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) is being adopted across education, from tutoring dialogue to the creation of learning materials~\cite{kasneci2023chatgpt}. For educational games and digital textbooks, text-to-image and multimodal models make the many small visual assets a lesson needs, such as fraction tiles, counting cards, and left--right puzzles, cheap to produce at scale. Unlike decorative art, these assets carry the concept being taught. Game-based learning research treats the visual design of a game as one of the elements through which it supports learning~\cite{plass2015foundations}, and many school concepts are defined by visual properties that can be checked. A unit fraction $1/b$, for example, is one of $b$ \emph{equal} parts of a whole~\cite{ccss2010math}, so a diagram whose parts differ in size misrepresents the concept, in a topic that many learners already find difficult~\cite{siegler2013fractions}.

Such errors are easy to generate and hard to catch, because the image remains plausible. Embedding-based scores such as CLIPScore~\cite{hessel2021clipscore} measure overall image--text compatibility rather than whether a quantitative rule holds, and CLIP-style models are largely insensitive to exact counts~\cite{paiss2023count}. Vision-language models (VLMs) used as judges~\cite{zheng2023judging,chen2024mllmjudge} are the natural next step, but VLMs miscount and misjudge elementary geometry~\cite{rahmanzadehgervi2024blind}, confuse basic spatial relations~\cite{kamath2023whatsup}, and report objects that are absent~\cite{li2023pope}. Their holistic verdicts also come without evidence that a teacher or a production pipeline could inspect. Finally, we are not aware of a controlled benchmark with exact ground truth for educational concept violations, so the reliability of any of these verifiers is unknown.

Experience with generated game assets shows how easily model-judged evaluation misleads~\cite{anon2026legibility}. In that study, scores from a single VLM judge shifted between identical scoring passes by as much as the smallest effect under study, an application programming interface (API) fault produced errors that a naive harness would have counted as wrong answers, and rankings of asset packs did not survive replacing one judge with another. Conclusions became trustworthy only once an answer key defined by executable game code was available and every judge had passed a battery of validity checks before scoring. Educational assets are a favorable setting for these lessons, because the answer key can be generated together with the asset.

This paper therefore asks: \emph{Can constraint-grounded multimodal verification reliably detect educational concept violations in AI-generated visual assets without requiring human evaluation?} Answering it requires meeting three challenges.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1. Educational constraint representation:} turning an abstract learning objective into constraints that a machine can check.
\item \textbf{C2. Visual-to-constraint grounding:} extracting verifiable visual evidence from a generated image instead of trusting a VLM's overall verdict.
\item \textbf{C3. Robust counterfactual verification:} detecting a local concept violation without being swayed by style or background.
\end{itemize}

We propose EduAssetVerify, which separates perception from judgment. A VLM reports what the image contains without being told what the constraints expect, producing a structured evidence graph. Deterministic measurement and a rule engine decide each formal constraint, and a VLM semantic verifier handles the constraints that resist formalization. An asset is accepted only when every constraint is positively verified, and the verifier answers \Uncertain{} when the evidence is insufficient or repeated readings disagree. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An educational constraint schema} for the cardinality, geometric, spatial, and semantic requirements of visual assets, with a consistency check on every constraint set (Section~\ref{sec:formulation}).
\item \textbf{A constraint-grounded multimodal verifier} that combines value-blind VLM grounding, deterministic measurement and rule checking with three-valued outcomes, violation localization, and abstention (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{A counterfactual educational asset benchmark} of 1{,}020 correct and minimally violated assets with programmatically verified labels and unseen-condition splits, evaluated under a protocol that turns known failure modes of model-judged evaluation into explicit guards (Sections~\ref{sec:benchmark} and~\ref{sec:setup}).
\end{enumerate}
On 900 test images with two VLM backbones from different model families, EduAssetVerify accepted none of the 2{,}700 evaluations of violated assets, whereas VLM judges accepted 8 to 9 of 1{,}350 under one backbone and 42 to 58 under the other, at the price of reviewing 1.5 to 3.1\% of assets. Two results bound the claim: under one backbone the judges are more accurate overall once abstentions count as errors, and a rule-only variant is at least as accurate as the full method, so the gain comes from measured, value-blind evidence rather than from the components layered on top of it.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Generative AI for Educational Visual Content}
Generative tools are being adopted to create and personalize learning materials, alongside recognized risks of inaccurate content~\cite{kasneci2023chatgpt}. Text-guided systems already produce scientific figures as editable code~\cite{belouadi2024automatikz} and game asset packs conditioned on gameplay structure~\cite{anon2026legibility}, yet their quality is still assessed with general image-quality and alignment scores, which do not encode the instructional rule an asset must obey.

\subsection{Vision-Language Models for Visual Verification}
Model judges grade generated content at scale~\cite{zheng2023judging}, and multimodal judges extend the practice to images, with documented biases and inconsistencies~\cite{chen2024mllmjudge}. Text-to-image evaluation decomposes a prompt into questions~\cite{cho2024dsg} or verifies counts, colors, and positions with object detectors~\cite{ghosh2023geneval}. Each check still ends in a model's answer, although VLMs fail elementary geometric tasks~\cite{rahmanzadehgervi2024blind}, struggle with spatial prepositions~\cite{kamath2023whatsup}, and hallucinate objects~\cite{li2023pope}. EduAssetVerify uses the VLM to extract evidence rather than to deliver the verdict.

\subsection{Constraint-Based and Neuro-Symbolic Verification}
Neuro-symbolic visual question answering parses a scene into a structured representation and executes a symbolic program on it~\cite{yi2018nsvqa}, visual programming composes perception modules through code written by a language model~\cite{gupta2023visprog,suris2023vipergpt}, and promptable segmentation supplies region masks on demand~\cite{kirillov2023sam}. EduAssetVerify adopts the parse-then-check structure for a different question: whether an image satisfies an explicit educational constraint set, with \Uncertain{} as an admissible answer. Generation--verification pipelines pair a generator with a critic~\cite{anon2026legibility}; EduAssetVerify could serve as such a critic, but is evaluated here as a verifier in its own right.

\subsection{Counterfactual and Metamorphic Evaluation}
Procedurally rendered scenes with exact ground truth enable diagnostic evaluation of visual reasoning~\cite{johnson2017clevr}. Contrast sets test models on minimally edited inputs~\cite{gardner2020contrast}, and benchmarks without such control can be solved through shortcuts~\cite{hsieh2023sugarcrepe}. Metamorphic testing checks relations between outputs for transformed inputs when no exact oracle exists~\cite{chen2018metamorphic}, and selective prediction lets a model abstain to lower its risk~\cite{geifman2017selective,whitehead2022reliable}. Our benchmark combines exact oracles with single-property violations and style transformations that must leave the verdict unchanged, and scores abstention as a risk--coverage trade-off.

\subsection{Research Gap}
In short, image-quality metrics do not test the concept, text--image similarity is insensitive to exact counts, proportions, and relations, VLM judges return verdicts without checkable evidence, rule-based checks lack the perception that raster images require, and general visual reasoning benchmarks lack educational constraints and controlled violation pairs. EduAssetVerify is not another image-quality score: it converts visual content into checkable evidence and verifies the instructional constraints on that evidence.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{Educational Asset Verification}
A learning objective $L$, such as ``represent one fourth,'' is turned into an asset generation prompt $P$, and a generative model renders an image $I$. The objective induces a constraint set $C=\{c_1,\ldots,c_n\}$ that every image teaching $L$ must satisfy. A verifier computes
\begin{equation}
V(I,C)\in\{\Valid,\Invalid,\Uncertain\},\label{eq:task}
\end{equation}
where \Uncertain{} means that the evidence does not support a reliable decision; in a production pipeline it routes the asset to a human reviewer instead of accepting or discarding it.

\subsection{Educational Constraint Representation}
Each constraint names a subject (an object set, a region, or a pair of entities), a predicate, an operator, a target value, and, for measured quantities, a tolerance. We distinguish four types:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cardinality} constraints fix object counts and set sizes.
\item \textbf{Geometric} constraints fix proportions, symmetry, angles, and region areas.
\item \textbf{Spatial} constraints fix orientation, arrangement, containment, and relative position.
\item \textbf{Semantic} constraints fix object categories, the concept shown, and the correspondence between labels and content.
\end{enumerate}
For the objective ``represent one fourth'' shown as a partitioned whole, the constraint set, in the naming of our schema, is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_{1/4}=\{\,&\cn{part\_count}=4,\ \cn{equal\_area},\\
&\cn{colored\_part\_count}=1,\\
&\cn{fraction\_value}=0.25\,\}.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:example}
\end{equation}
The last constraint follows from the first three; stating it lets the parser check consistency, so that ``one third, shown as one of four equal parts'' yields an unsatisfiable set and is rejected before any image is verified. The schema has 15 constraint names across the three domains of Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}.

\subsection{Verification Objectives}
A verifier is evaluated on \emph{validity}, deciding $V$ correctly; \emph{violation localization}, naming the violated constraint and the object or region responsible; and \emph{robustness}, keeping both across visual styles, asset templates, and error types.

\section{EduAssetVerify}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Framework Overview}
In Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}, a parser turns the learning objective into a constraint graph (Section~\ref{sec:parser}), a VLM reads the image into an evidence graph without seeing target values (Section~\ref{sec:grounding}), rules and a semantic verifier decide each constraint (Section~\ref{sec:verify}), and the decisions are combined into a verdict with a violation report (Sections~\ref{sec:aggregate} and~\ref{sec:abstain}). Two design rules adopted from the game-asset study~\cite{anon2026legibility} run through every stage: the component that reads the image never sees the answer, and no single model reading is trusted. A third requires every decision to cite its evidence.

\subsection{Educational Constraint Parser}\label{sec:parser}
The parser returns a constraint graph whose nodes are entities (the whole, its parts, object sets) and constraints. A language model, the same backbone as the verifier, transcribes every stated requirement into the schema and is told to keep conflicting requirements rather than resolve them. A deterministic check then rejects unsatisfiable sets: conflicting values, more colored parts than parts, a fraction or label that disagrees with the part counts, a numeral that disagrees with the object count, and cyclic spatial relations. Two regimes are evaluated separately, so that an extraction error is never reported as an image-verification error: \emph{gold constraints}, authored with each benchmark template, isolate perception and verification, and \emph{extracted constraints}, produced by the parser from $L$, measure the full pipeline.

\subsection{Value-Blind Visual Grounding}\label{sec:grounding}
A VLM converts the image into an evidence graph
\begin{equation}
G_I=(O_I,R_I,A_I),\label{eq:evidence}
\end{equation}
with detected objects and regions $O_I$ (category and box), their relations $R_I$, and attributes $A_I$ such as color, text, and area. Four choices make $G_I$ evidence rather than opinion.

\emph{The prompt is value-blind.} It lists the entity and attribute vocabulary of $C$ (for example, \emph{whole}, \emph{part}, \emph{colored}, \emph{apple}) but none of the operators or target values. A model asked to confirm that four equal parts are present can agree without looking; a model asked to list the parts and mark the colored ones has to look.

\emph{The answer space is open and global.} Objects are returned as lists rather than yes/no answers, with categories from a vocabulary covering the whole domain; a per-item candidate list that holds little besides the right answer made every item trivially answerable in the game-asset study~\cite{anon2026legibility}. Boxes are requested in each model's native coordinate frame (Section~\ref{sec:guards}).

\emph{Geometry is measured, not estimated.} Because VLMs misjudge precise geometry~\cite{rahmanzadehgervi2024blind}, deterministic operators compute it. The background is estimated from the image border, a divided shape is segmented into the regions bounded by color edges inside the box the VLM reports, objects are counted as foreground components, and mirror overlap and arrow orientation are computed from the foreground mask. The operators use no knowledge of the style's palette, and every attribute records whether it was read by the VLM or measured.

\emph{Readings are repeated.} Grounding runs $m=3$ times independently, and disagreement becomes uncertainty (Section~\ref{sec:abstain}). Grounding sees only pixels, never the renderer's parameters, file names, or metadata.

\subsection{Hybrid Constraint Verification}\label{sec:verify}
Each constraint $c_j$ receives a status $s_j\in\{\sT,\sF,\sU\}$: satisfied, violated, or undetermined.

\emph{Deterministic verification} handles the formal constraints: exact object counts, fraction and ratio values, spatial relations, geometric consistency, and logical combinations. Each checker states its preconditions, and a measured quantity whose deviation from its required value is $d_j$ receives
\begin{equation}
s_j=\begin{cases}\sT, & d_j\le\epsilon_j^{\sT},\\ \sF, & d_j\ge\epsilon_j^{\sF},\\ \sU, & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}\label{eq:zones}
\end{equation}
with tolerances $\epsilon_j^{\sT}<\epsilon_j^{\sF}$ fixed on the development split. Count checks compare the matching object set with the target, and spatial checks compare region centroids with a margin proportional to object size. A failed precondition, such as parts that cannot be segmented, gives $\sU$.

\emph{VLM semantic verification} handles the constraints that resist formalization: semantic correctness, object-category consistency, grounding of the educational concept, and visually ambiguous relations. Each is posed as an open question about specific nodes of $G_I$, asked $m$ times: text is re-read from a crop around the label that grounding located, and categories from a numbered montage of the grounded objects. The rule engine compares the answers with the target values.

The semantic verifier never overrides a measured quantity, and insufficient evidence yields $\sU$ rather than a guess presented as a formal guarantee.

\subsection{Violation Detection and Localization}\label{sec:aggregate}
With the order $\sF<\sU<\sT$, the verdict is the strong Kleene conjunction of all constraint statuses:
\begin{equation}
V(I,C)=\begin{cases}\Valid, & \min_j s_j=\sT,\\ \Invalid, & \min_j s_j=\sF,\\ \Uncertain, & \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\label{eq:verdict}
\end{equation}
One well-evidenced violation rejects an asset, whereas acceptance requires every constraint to be positively verified, because accepting an asset that teaches the wrong concept costs more than reviewing a correct one. For each constraint, the report records status, evidence (graph nodes and measured values), violation type, affected object or region (the \emph{witness}), and confidence; an unequal partition, for instance, is witnessed by the part whose area deviates most.

\subsection{Verification Confidence and Abstention}\label{sec:abstain}
The confidence $q_j$ of a status averages an agreement weight over the $m$ readings: 0 for a reading that disagrees with the majority status, 1 for one that agrees, and for measured quantities the agreeing reading's distance to the nearest threshold in \eqref{eq:zones}, relative to a fixed margin and capped at 1. A status is kept only if $q_j\ge\tau$ and is otherwise replaced by $\sU$, so that disagreement surfaces as uncertainty; the verdict then follows from \eqref{eq:verdict}. The threshold $\tau$ trades false decisions for abstentions, and ranking answers by $\min_j q_j$ traces a risk--coverage curve~\cite{geifman2017selective}.

\section{Counterfactual Educational Asset Benchmark}\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{Design Principles}
The benchmark has programmatically verified ground truth, controlled counterfactual pairs, three educational domains, visual diversity, and test conditions unseen during development.

\subsection{Educational Task Categories}
\emph{Task A, fraction understanding,} covers equal partition, colored fractions, and part-to-whole representation in circles, strips, and grids, with and without a written fraction. \emph{Task B, object counting and classification,} covers exact counts, category-based counts among distractor objects, and two groups separated by a line, with and without a written numeral. \emph{Task C, spatial reasoning,} covers left and right, above and below, the order of three objects, mirror symmetry, and a quarter-turn rotation. Each task has five templates, three for development and two held out.

\subsection{Counterfactual Error Generation}
Each violated asset is derived from a correct one by a single controlled change,
\begin{equation}
I^{-}=T_k(I^{+}),\label{eq:cf}
\end{equation}
where $T_k$ is the $k$th error operator. Nine operators cover seven error types: missing object, extra object, incorrect proportion (unequal partition, wrong colored count, wrong part count), incorrect label (fraction labels and numerals), spatial relation reversal (pairs, rows, and rotations), broken symmetry, and semantic mismatch (Fig.~\ref{fig:pairs}). The benchmark consists of pairs
\begin{equation}
D=\{(I^{+},I^{-},C,y^{+},y^{-})\},\label{eq:pairs}
\end{equation}
in which both images share the constraint set $C$, with $y^{+}=\Valid$ and $y^{-}=\Invalid$. Operators act on the scene specification, and an assertion re-evaluates $C$ on both specifications: exactly the targeted constraint must change status, which makes each pair diagnostic. Perturbations exceed a minimum fixed before any verifier ran (an unequal partition changes two parts' areas by 35 to 60\%, and a broken symmetry moves an element by more than its size), and correct assets are exact by construction, so no label depends on measurement noise.

\subsection{Dataset Construction}\label{sec:construction}
Table~\ref{tab:data} gives the composition: 1{,}020 images in 510 pairs, of which 900 images form the test set. A procedural renderer in Python draws each scene specification as a $512\times512$ image and as scalable vector graphics (SVG), in one of seven styles: three development styles and four unseen ones (a dark theme, thick pastel outlines, a hand-drawn wobble, and textured paper). Before any verifier is asked a question, the answer key is checked~\cite{anon2026legibility}: an independent script recomputes all 2{,}720 constraint labels from the pixels, using only the style's palette and never the specification, and an item enters the benchmark only if both agree. During development this check rejected items whose rendering contradicted their specification, such as a label touching its shape, and each rejection led to a renderer fix; the final build discards none. Images carry random names and no metadata, the specification stays with the evaluator, and a fixed seed rebuilds the benchmark byte for byte, which a test asserts.

\subsection{Data Splits and Generalization}
No component is trained; the development split serves only to design prompts and to fix tolerances and~$\tau$. The test conditions are in-domain; unseen template, from the six held-out templates; unseen error combination, two composed operators, which development never shows; and unseen style, which re-renders in-domain test pairs in the four unseen styles and so also measures style invariance. Spatial development templates carry a single relation, so the spatial domain has more in-domain pairs instead of combinations.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Research Questions}
The experiments address four research questions (RQs).
\textbf{RQ1:} Does constraint-grounded verification outperform conventional VLM-based judging in detecting educational visual errors?
\textbf{RQ2:} Can EduAssetVerify correctly localize constraint violations?
\textbf{RQ3:} Does the framework generalize across educational tasks, visual styles, and asset templates?
\textbf{RQ4:} What is the trade-off between verification accuracy, abstention, latency, and computational cost?

\subsection{Baseline Methods}
We compare six methods. \emph{Text--image similarity} thresholds CLIPScore~\cite{hessel2021clipscore} between image and objective, with ViT-B/32, on the development split. The \emph{VLM direct judge} asks whether the image is correct, the \emph{chain-of-thought (CoT) judge} reasons step by step before its verdict, and the \emph{structured judge} checks each constraint of $C$ in turn. The \emph{rule-only validator} receives the same evidence graph as EduAssetVerify but has neither the semantic verifier nor abstention, and it maps undetermined statuses to \Invalid. All VLM-based methods use the same backbones, images, and objective and constraint information, so that differences reflect the verification strategy rather than model capability.


\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\emph{Error detection.} \Invalid{} is the positive class, and F1 is computed over answered items. The false acceptance rate (FAR) is the fraction of violated assets judged \Valid, and the false rejection rate (FRR) the fraction of correct assets judged \Invalid, both over all items, so that abstaining never counts as acceptance.

\emph{Constraint level.} Violation type accuracy checks that every reported violation is a perturbed constraint, and localization accuracy counts a witness as correct when its intersection over union with a perturbed region is at least 0.5, for the six operators that leave a place in the image (not a missing or extra copy of identical objects).

\emph{Counterfactual.} Pairwise discrimination is the fraction of pairs with $V(I^{+})=\Valid$ and $V(I^{-})=\Invalid$; style invariance is the fraction of re-styled assets whose verdict equals that on the same scene in its development style.

\emph{Reliability and efficiency.} We report abstention, the area under the risk--coverage curve (AURC, abstentions ranked last with a forced guess), summed call latency, and estimated cost.

\subsection{Measurement Protocol and Guards}\label{sec:guards}
Before any of its numbers is reported, each VLM backbone must pass, as grounder and as judge, a five-check validity battery adapted from the game-asset study~\cite{anon2026legibility}: protocol compliance, test--retest agreement, invariance to permuted options and vocabulary, a blank-image control (grounding must return no objects and judges must fall to chance), and accuracy above a constant-majority predictor. Every configuration then runs in three independent evaluation passes, separate from the $m$ readings, reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation (sd); a difference is claimed only if it exceeds two standard errors under every backbone. Table~\ref{tab:guards} lists the guarded failure modes.

A new failure mode surfaced on the development split, before any test image was scored. Claude answered bounding boxes in image pixels in 84\% of readings although asked for 0 to 1000 units, which moved every crop of the semantic verifier off its target and dropped the development accuracy of EduAssetVerify to 63.1\%. Each backbone is now asked in its native frame, and protocol compliance checks that reported boxes land on the drawn content; the corrected pipeline reached 98.9\%. Rows 1, 5, and 6 of Table~\ref{tab:guards} are executable checks that stop a run; the others are rules of the scoring and reporting code.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\emph{Models:} \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash} through the Gemini API (thinking budget 1{,}024 tokens, temperature 0.7) and Claude Sonnet~5 (\texttt{global.anthropic.claude-sonnet-5}) through Amazon Bedrock, with default sampling because the model rejects a temperature parameter; the same backbone parses constraints in the extracted regime. Both pass the battery in both roles: grounding returned no objects for every blank image, and judges fell from at least 83\% accuracy on real images to the chance level of 50\% on blank ones. \emph{Settings,} fixed on the development split: $512\times512$ images, $m=3$, $\tau=0.6$ (every $\tau$ from 0.34 to 0.6 gives identical development results), equal-area zones $(0.10, 0.20)$ of relative deviation, mirror-overlap zones $(0.92, 0.82)$, and a relational margin of a quarter of the mean object size. Rules and measurement are written in Python with NumPy and OpenCV and run on a CPU server; prompts, code, and model responses will be released.

\subsection{Overall Verification Performance}
Table~\ref{tab:main} answers RQ1. EduAssetVerify accepted none of the violated assets under either backbone: 0 of 1{,}350 violated evaluations per backbone, against 8 to 9 for the three judges under Gemini and 42 to 58 under Claude. The difference exceeds two standard errors for every judge under both backbones, and F1 on answered items is 99.8 and 100.0 against at most 99.5 and 97.7 for the judges. CLIPScore is at chance (50.9\% accuracy). The cost of the zero is abstention: EduAssetVerify sends 3.1\% (Gemini) and 1.5\% (Claude) of assets to review. Counting those as errors, its accuracy of 96.7\% under Gemini is below the judges' 99.1 to 99.5\%, so on accuracy alone it outperforms the judges only under Claude (98.5\% against 95.7 to 97.7\%). \subsection{Performance across Educational Domains}

\subsection{Ablation Study and Localization}
Each configuration in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} changes one setting of the full method and reuses its stored readings, except that the variant without the rule engine adds one VLM call per reading. Without the rule engine, the VLM decides each constraint from a text description of the same evidence graph, including the measured values; without measurement, counts and geometry come from the VLM's own boxes; without semantic verification, semantic constraints are decided from the grounding reading alone; and without abstention, $\tau=0$ and undetermined statuses map to \Invalid. Measurement matters most: removing it raises FAR to 1.9\% and FRR to 8.3\% under Claude. Letting the VLM apply the rules to the same evidence raises FRR to 1.6\% and 3.8\%. The semantic verifier shows no measurable benefit, and abstention prevents no false acceptance; what it buys is fewer false rejections (0.4\% instead of 2.0\% under Gemini) at the price of 3.1\% reviews.

For RQ2, over its correct rejections of localizable errors, EduAssetVerify names only perturbed constraints in 99.6\% (Gemini) and 100\% (Claude) of cases, and its witness box hits the perturbed region in 94.2\% and 85.5\%. The misses have two causes. For broken symmetry, the mirror test cannot tell the moved element from its partner, and the witness names either. For text labels under Claude, the reported box lies inside the ground-truth box, which is estimated from font metrics, but covers less than half of it.

\subsection{Computational Efficiency}
Table~\ref{tab:cost} answers RQ4 together with the AURC of Table~\ref{tab:main}. \section{Discussion}

\subsection{Implications for Generative AI in Education}
Because acceptance requires positive evidence for every constraint, the verifier can serve as a quality gate for automated material production, and its abstentions define the review queue for teachers or designers. On this benchmark the gate let no wrong concept through at a review load of 1.5 to 3.1\%, whereas judges let through up to 4.3\% of violations, concentrated in exactly the proportions and symmetries that learners must read correctly. As with the critic loop of the game-asset study~\cite{anon2026legibility}, abstention changes review load rather than detection, so its rate is a cost to report rather than to hide. More broadly, separating perception from judgment offers a route to trustworthy multimodal verification wherever requirements can be stated explicitly.

\subsection{Advantages of Constraint-Grounded Verification}
The framework separates aesthetic quality from instructional correctness, turns a free-form visual judgment into inspectable evidence, makes every rejection reproducible from its report, and needs no raters, since benchmark labels come from the renderer. The experiments locate the advantage: value-blind grounding and deterministic measurement, which the rule-only validator shares. The semantic verifier and abstention, which we expected to matter, did not improve detection on these assets.

\subsection{Limitations}
This work does not measure learning outcomes and makes no claim about learners' understanding or achievement. It covers only concepts whose correctness can be formalized as constraints, and many qualities of good teaching material, such as clarity or cultural fit, cannot be fully decided by program rules. Perception errors of the VLM still propagate into rule checks; repetition and abstention limit their effect but do not remove it. The benchmark is procedurally rendered: we built no cross-generator condition, because outputs of image generators come without labels that can be confirmed without human raters, so the domain gap to generated and real educational content remains untested. Under Gemini the judges are near ceiling, which leaves little room to separate methods on accuracy. Costs rest on assumed token prices, and results depend on specific model versions, which change over time, so we report the versions and will release the evaluation code and model responses for re-running.

\subsection{Future Work}
Next steps include harder concepts and diagrams, a labeled set of generator outputs, content that mixes text, images, and interaction, automatic repair driven by the violation report, and deployment studies with teachers in real courses.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented EduAssetVerify, a constraint-grounded framework for verifying AI-generated visual assets for education. It contributes a structured representation of educational constraints with a consistency check, a verification method that combines value-blind VLM grounding with deterministic measurement, rules, and abstention, and a reproducible counterfactual benchmark with programmatically verified labels, evaluated under a protocol that guards against known failure modes of model-judged evaluation. On 900 test images, two model families, and three passes, it accepted no violated asset, while VLM judges accepted 0.6 to 4.3\%; it localized 85.5 to 94.2\% of the localizable errors it rejected, kept zero false acceptance across unseen templates, error combinations, and styles, and did so at four to nine times the judges' cost and a review load of 1.5 to 3.1\%. Contrary to our design expectation, the semantic verifier and abstention added no detection benefit: the evidence and its measurement carry the result. The framework applies to concepts that can be stated as constraints, and it makes no claim about learning outcomes, which require studies with learners.
