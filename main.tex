\begin{abstract}
Generative models can now produce the visual material of an entire curriculum, which moves the bottleneck of educational content from production to verification: an image that looks right can still teach the wrong concept, such as a fraction drawn with unequal parts. Existing evaluators cannot close this gap. Similarity and aesthetic scores ignore the instructional rule, and vision-language model judges return holistic verdicts that are unreliable and carry no checkable evidence. The challenge is to verify instructional correctness at scale, without human raters, and with a reliability that can itself be measured. We present EduAssetVerify, which separates perception from judgment: learning objectives become formal constraints, a vision-language model only reports what an image contains without seeing the expected answer, geometry is measured deterministically, and three-valued rules decide each constraint, abstaining instead of guessing. To measure reliability without raters, we build a counterfactual benchmark in which every correct asset is paired with a minimally altered violation and every label is verified against the rendered pixels. On 900 test images and two model families, EduAssetVerify accepted no violated asset, whereas the judges accepted up to 4.3 percent, at the cost of sending 1.5 to 3.1 percent of assets to review.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Generative artificial intelligence is turning learning materials from a scarce craft into an abundant resource: a single prompt can now illustrate an entire unit, personalized to each learner~\cite{kasneci2023chatgpt}. Abundance moves the bottleneck from production to verification. In educational games, visual assets carry the concept being taught~\cite{plass2015foundations}, and many concepts are defined by checkable visual properties: a unit fraction $1/b$ is one of $b$ \emph{equal} parts of a whole~\cite{ccss2010math}. An asset that looks right but breaks such a rule silently teaches a misconception, in topics that learners already find difficult~\cite{siegler2013fractions}, and human review cannot keep pace with generation.

Automatic evaluators do not close this gap. Embedding-based similarity scores~\cite{hessel2021clipscore} measure overall image--text compatibility and are largely insensitive to exact counts~\cite{paiss2023count}. Vision-language models (VLMs) used as judges~\cite{zheng2023judging,chen2024mllmjudge} miscount and misjudge elementary geometry~\cite{rahmanzadehgervi2024blind}, confuse spatial relations~\cite{kamath2023whatsup}, and report absent objects~\cite{li2023pope}, and their holistic verdicts carry no evidence that a teacher could inspect. Model-judged evaluation also misleads its users: in a study of generated game assets~\cite{anon2026legibility}, scores shifted between identical passes by as much as the effect under study, a deprecated request parameter made one judge fail on every call, and rankings did not survive a change of judge. No benchmark with exact ground truth measures how reliable such verifiers are on educational content.

Verifying instructional correctness therefore poses three challenges:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Learning objectives are abstract, so they must be turned into requirements that a machine can check and whose consistency can be tested.
\item VLMs perceive well but judge poorly, so their perception must yield checkable evidence, without letting them see the answer they are asked to verify.
\item Violations are local and subtle, so a verifier must catch one wrong part in any style or template, and its reliability must be measured without human labels.
\end{enumerate}
We address them with EduAssetVerify (Figs.~\ref{fig:teaser} and~\ref{fig:overview}), a general recipe for evidence-based verification that separates perception from judgment: models report what they see, deterministic measurement and rules decide, and the verifier abstains rather than guesses. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A formal constraint schema that makes instructional correctness machine-checkable, with a consistency check that rejects contradictory objectives before verification.
\item A verifier that combines value-blind grounding, deterministic measurement, and three-valued rules; on 900 test images it accepted no violated asset under either of two model families.
\item A counterfactual benchmark with programmatically verified labels and a measurement protocol for model-based verifiers, showing that VLM judges fail precisely where correctness must be measured, not recognized.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Generative AI for Educational Visual Content}
Generative tools are being adopted to create learning materials, alongside risks of inaccurate content~\cite{kasneci2023chatgpt}. Text-guided systems produce scientific figures as code~\cite{belouadi2024automatikz} and game assets conditioned on gameplay structure~\cite{anon2026legibility}, yet quality is still assessed with general image-quality and alignment scores that do not encode the instructional rule an asset must obey.

\subsection{Vision-Language Models for Visual Verification}
Model judges grade generated content at scale~\cite{zheng2023judging}, and multimodal judges extend the practice to images, with documented biases~\cite{chen2024mllmjudge}. Text-to-image evaluation decomposes a prompt into questions~\cite{cho2024dsg} or checks counts and positions with detectors~\cite{ghosh2023geneval}, but each check still ends in a model's answer. EduAssetVerify uses the VLM to extract evidence, not to deliver the verdict.

\subsection{Constraint-Based and Neuro-Symbolic Verification}
Neuro-symbolic visual question answering executes symbolic programs on parsed scenes~\cite{yi2018nsvqa}, and visual programming composes perception modules through generated code~\cite{gupta2023visprog,suris2023vipergpt}. EduAssetVerify adopts this parse-then-check structure to decide whether an image satisfies an explicit educational constraint set, with \Uncertain{} as an admissible answer.

\subsection{Counterfactual and Selective Evaluation}
Procedurally rendered scenes with exact ground truth enable diagnostic evaluation~\cite{johnson2017clevr}, contrast sets test minimally edited inputs~\cite{gardner2020contrast}, and uncontrolled benchmarks can be solved through shortcuts~\cite{hsieh2023sugarcrepe}. Metamorphic testing relates the outputs of transformed inputs~\cite{chen2018metamorphic}, and selective prediction lets a model abstain to lower its risk~\cite{geifman2017selective,whitehead2022reliable}. Our benchmark combines exact oracles, single-property violations, and style changes that must leave the verdict unchanged.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:formulation}

A learning objective $L$, such as ``represent one fourth,'' induces a constraint set $C=\{c_1,\ldots,c_n\}$ that every image $I$ teaching $L$ must satisfy. A verifier computes
\begin{equation}
V(I,C)\in\{\Valid,\Invalid,\Uncertain\},\label{eq:task}
\end{equation}
where \Uncertain{} routes the asset to a human reviewer. Beyond deciding $V$, a verifier should name the violated constraint and the affected region, and remain correct across visual styles, asset templates, and error types.

Each constraint names a subject, a predicate, an operator, a target value, and, for measured quantities, a tolerance. The schema has 15 constraint names of four types: cardinality (counts), geometric (areas and symmetry), spatial (relative position and orientation), and semantic (categories and labels). For one fourth shown as a divided whole,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_{1/4}=\{\,&\cn{part\_count}=4,\ \cn{equal\_area},\\
&\cn{colored\_part\_count}=1,\\
&\cn{fraction\_value}=0.25\,\}.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:example}
\end{equation}
The last constraint follows from the others; stating it lets a consistency check reject an objective such as ``one third, shown as one of four equal parts'' before any image is verified.

\section{EduAssetVerify}\label{sec:method}

Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} follows the asset of Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser} through every module.


\subsection{Constraint Parser}\label{sec:parser}
A language model, the same backbone as the verifier, transcribes every requirement stated in $L$ into the schema and keeps conflicting requirements rather than resolving them. A deterministic check then rejects unsatisfiable sets, such as conflicting values, a fraction or label that disagrees with the part counts, or cyclic spatial relations. Gold constraints, authored with each template, are evaluated separately from extracted ones, so that an extraction error is never counted as an image-verification error.

\subsection{Value-Blind Grounding and Measurement}\label{sec:grounding}
A VLM reads the image into an evidence graph $G_I=(O_I,R_I,A_I)$ of objects and regions with boxes, their relations, and attributes such as color and text. The prompt names the vocabulary of $C$ (for example, \emph{part}, \emph{colored}, \emph{apple}) but no target values, because a model asked to confirm four equal parts can agree without looking. Objects are listed rather than confirmed, with categories from the whole domain vocabulary. Because VLMs misjudge precise geometry~\cite{rahmanzadehgervi2024blind}, deterministic operators measure it without knowledge of the palette: a divided shape is segmented into the regions bounded by color edges inside its reported box, objects are counted as foreground components, and mirror overlap and arrow orientation are computed from the foreground mask. Grounding is repeated $m=3$ times.

\subsection{Hybrid Verification and Abstention}\label{sec:verify}
Each constraint $c_j$ receives a status $s_j\in\{\sT,\sF,\sU\}$. Rules decide the formal constraints: a measured deviation $d_j$ receives
\begin{equation}
s_j=\sT \text{ if } d_j\le\epsilon_j^{\sT},\quad \sF \text{ if } d_j\ge\epsilon_j^{\sF},\quad \sU \text{ otherwise},\label{eq:zones}
\end{equation}
and a failed precondition, such as parts that cannot be segmented, gives $\sU$. Semantic constraints are posed as open questions about nodes of $G_I$: labels are re-read from a crop, categories from a numbered montage of the grounded objects, and the rules compare the answers with the targets. The confidence $q_j$ averages the agreement of the $m$ readings with the majority status, weighting measured readings by their distance to the nearest threshold of~\eqref{eq:zones}, and a status with $q_j<\tau$ becomes $\sU$. With $\sF<\sU<\sT$, the verdict is the three-valued conjunction
\begin{equation}
V(I,C)=\begin{cases}\Valid, & \min_j s_j=\sT,\\ \Invalid, & \min_j s_j=\sF,\\ \Uncertain, & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}\label{eq:verdict}
\end{equation}
so one well-evidenced violation rejects an asset, whereas acceptance requires every constraint to be verified. The report lists each status with its evidence, measured values, and witness region, such as the part whose area deviates most.

\section{Counterfactual Educational Asset Benchmark}\label{sec:benchmark}

The benchmark covers three tasks with five templates each, three for development and two held out: fraction understanding (circles, strips, and grids, optionally labeled), object counting and classification (exact counts, distractors, two groups, optional numeral), and spatial reasoning (left--right, above--below, three-object order, mirror symmetry, and quarter-turn rotation). Each violated asset $I^{-}=T_k(I^{+})$ applies one of nine operators to a correct asset, covering seven error types: missing object, extra object, incorrect proportion, incorrect label, spatial relation reversal, broken symmetry, and semantic mismatch. Operators act on the scene specification, and an assertion checks that exactly the targeted constraint changes status. A procedural renderer draws each scene at $512\times512$ pixels in one of seven styles, and before any verifier runs, an independent script recomputes every label from the pixels; an item is kept only if both answers agree~\cite{anon2026legibility}. The benchmark has 1{,}020 images: 120 for development, which fixes prompts and thresholds, and 900 for testing, 300 per task, under four conditions: in-domain, unseen templates, combinations of two operators, and four unseen styles (Fig.~\ref{fig:qual} and Appendix~\ref{app:bench}).

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

We ask whether EduAssetVerify detects violations better than VLM judges (RQ1), localizes them (RQ2), generalizes to unseen conditions (RQ3), and at what cost (RQ4).

\emph{Baselines.} CLIPScore~\cite{hessel2021clipscore} with ViT-B/32, thresholded on the development split; a VLM direct judge; a chain-of-thought (CoT) judge; a structured judge that checks each constraint in turn; and a rule-only validator that uses our evidence without the semantic verifier and abstention, mapping $\sU$ to \Invalid. All VLM methods receive the same images, objective, and constraints.

\emph{Metrics.} With \Invalid{} as the positive class, the false acceptance rate (FAR) is the fraction of violated assets judged \Valid{} and the false rejection rate (FRR) the fraction of correct assets judged \Invalid; abstention counts against accuracy but never as acceptance. A witness localizes correctly at an intersection over union of at least 0.5 with a perturbed region.

\emph{Protocol.} Before scoring, each backbone passed a five-check validity battery~\cite{anon2026legibility} as grounder and as judge: protocol compliance, test--retest agreement, permutation invariance, a blank-image control, and accuracy above a majority guess. On the development split, the battery revealed that Claude answered boxes in pixels although asked for normalized units, so each model is asked for boxes in its native coordinate frame. Every configuration ran three independent passes; we report means and claim a difference only if it exceeds two standard errors under both backbones.

\emph{Models and settings.} \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash} (thinking budget 1{,}024 tokens, temperature 0.7) and Claude Sonnet~5 through Amazon Bedrock (default sampling), each also serving as parser; $m=3$, $\tau=0.6$, equal-area zones $(0.10, 0.20)$, and mirror-overlap zones $(0.92, 0.82)$, all fixed on the development split.

\emph{Ablation and localization (RQ2).} Measurement matters most (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}): without it, Claude's FAR rises to 1.9\% and its FRR to 8.3\%. Letting the VLM apply the rules to the same evidence raises FRR to 1.6\% and 3.8\%. The semantic verifier shows no measurable benefit, and abstention prevents no false acceptance; it turns false rejections into reviews. Over correct rejections of localizable errors, the reported violations are perturbed constraints in 99.6\% and 100\% of cases, and the witness hits the perturbed region in 94.2\% and 85.5\%. The misses come from symmetry, where the moved element and its partner are indistinguishable, and from text boxes smaller than the ground truth.

\emph{Extracted constraints.} With parser-produced constraints, the pipeline accepted 2 of 1{,}350 violated evaluations under Gemini and none under Claude (Table~\ref{tab:main}, last row). The parsers reproduced the gold set for 83.8\% and 79.0\% of objectives; both false acceptances trace to parses that dropped ``and nothing else.'' The consistency check rejected 88.9\% and 100\% of 60 contradictory objectives without false alarms. A first run on the test split exposed a vocabulary mismatch (the divided whole parsed as a required object); we corrected the parser instruction on the development split and report the second run.

\emph{Cost (RQ4).} EduAssetVerify makes 4.9 VLM calls per asset, three readings plus re-readings, with 12 to 16 s of summed latency, and costs 4.2 to 9.2 times as much as a single judge call: USD 4.62 and 25.16 per 1{,}000 assets, against 0.84 to 1.11 and 2.73 to 5.50, at assumed token rates. Under Gemini, 54 of the 84 abstentions followed a malformed reply, which occurred in 2.2\% of readings.


\emph{Qualitative analysis.} In Fig.~\ref{fig:qual}, all three judges accepted an unequal partition whose colored parts keep their exact share, the misconception the operator models. The false rejection arose because a label crop also contained the circle's outline, and the sketch-style figure was sent to review because its mirror overlap of 0.88 lies between the zones.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

EduAssetVerify can gate automated material production, with its abstentions as the review queue for teachers or designers. Because its advantage over the judges lies in measured proportions and symmetries, the semantic verifier and abstention, which we expected to matter, are optional on assets like ours: the rule-only configuration was as safe, more accurate, and cheaper.

We do not measure learning outcomes, and only concepts expressible as constraints are covered. The benchmark is procedurally rendered: we built no cross-generator condition, because generator outputs lack labels that can be confirmed without raters. Under Gemini, the judges are near ceiling, which leaves little room to separate methods on accuracy, and costs rest on assumed token prices.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
EduAssetVerify shows that the instructional correctness of generated visuals can be verified with checkable evidence and without human labels. Asking a VLM for evidence rather than verdicts, measuring what can be measured, and letting three-valued rules decide eliminated false acceptances under two model families on our benchmark, where VLM judges accepted up to 4.3\% of violated assets, at the price of reviewing 1.5 to 3.1\% of assets and a four- to ninefold cost. The same recipe applies wherever generated content must satisfy explicit requirements. Future work will extend the schema to harder concepts and diagrams, build a labeled set of generator outputs, use violation reports to repair assets automatically, and study the effect of verified assets with teachers and learners.
