\begin{abstract}
Existing multi-image generation methods primarily target visual consistency.
Game asset production additionally requires \emph{gameplay consistency}.
Generated assets must preserve their roles, affordances, and progression semantics while sharing
a coherent visual style, and each asset must stay readable at board scale and ship as an
engine-ready cutout inside the pack.
Prior text-to-image and set-consistency methods optimize aesthetics, identity, or set-level
style, yet they do not treat gameplay function as a first-class constraint, and they do not check
acceptance inside a playable engine loop.
We formalize \emph{Gameplay-Consistent Asset Pack Generation} and present \textbf{GAPGen}, a
gameplay-aware asset pack generation system for Match-3 art with ontology planning, consistency
generation under dual visual references, and hybrid programmatic and VLM verification.
GAPGen conditions generation on a gameplay ontology so that families, affordances, and stage
progression are enforced rather than left to chance, and its critic separates visual-style checks
from gameplay-consistency checks.
In a Fruit-theme ablation over three seeds on twelve gameplay-critical assets, programmatic-only
stacks accept nearly all candidates in one pass, while adding the gameplay-consistency critic
spends more iterations and surfaces a variable \texttt{needs\_review} fraction, showing that
enforcing gameplay consistency adds targeted friction rather than maximizing accept rate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

  \centering

  \caption{A Fruit-theme Match-3 asset pack generated by GAPGen in a unified 3D-cartoon style.}

% Part A — general task -> specific setting
Text-to-image models make single images cheap to produce, and recent multi-image methods push
for identity or style consistency across a set~\cite{jia2025t2is,consistyle2025}, treating
consistency as a visual property.
Casual games, however, rarely need an isolated illustration.
They need a \emph{structured asset pack} of dozens of sprites that share a rendering language,
communicate distinct gameplay roles at roughly \(70\)px cell size, and import cleanly into an
engine.
Instead, such a pack must be \emph{gameplay-consistent}, meaning every asset preserves its role,
affordance, and progression semantics while sharing one style.
Concretely, we study this in a Match-3 game (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}), where assets include
color-matched elements, directional power-ups, multi-stage obstacles, and low-contrast board
backgrounds.

% Part B — why the task is hard
Gameplay consistency is hard for three reasons that visual consistency alone does not address.
First, consistency must respect \emph{function}.
For instance, a beautiful sprite that hides a horizontal-clear affordance, collapses stage
progression, or fails chromakey cutout is visually fine yet gameplay-inconsistent.
Second, consistency is \emph{hierarchical} rather than flat.
That is, assets must cohere within families, stay distinct across families, and evolve in discrete
steps across damage stages, all while preserving global style.
Third, consistency must survive a \emph{theme swap}.
In particular, when objects change, roles and affordances must stay stable, so left-to-right
readability or color identity cannot be lost just because a lemon becomes a fish.

% Part C — novelty
To address these challenges, we propose \textbf{GAPGen}, a gameplay-aware asset pack generation
system that treats the asset ontology as a first-class input and turns gameplay consistency into
an enforceable constraint.

% What we evaluate
We evaluate GAPGen with a Fruit-theme ablation over three seeds
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), using cross-model automatic proxies for readability and
preference, with a human study left as future work.
Empirically, programmatic-only stacks accept nearly every candidate in one iteration, while the
full gameplay-consistency critic spends more retries and flags a variable fraction of sprites for
human review at \(0.11 \pm 0.10\), showing that enforcing gameplay consistency adds friction where
a human should look rather than chasing a higher accept rate.

In summary, we contribute:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item A formulation of gameplay-consistent asset pack generation, with layered success criteria
        that separate production readiness, visual consistency, and gameplay consistency.
  \item GAPGen, a pipeline of ontology planning, consistency generation under dual references, and
        verification whose critic scores visual style and gameplay consistency on separate axes.
  \item A cross-model evaluation that isolates critic self-preference by having one model judge
        another model's outputs, complemented by a model-independent cohesion signal, through which
        an ablation shows that the critic redistributes friction rather than maximizing accept rate.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =============================================================================

\paragraph{Consistent multi-image generation.}
Text-to-ImageSet benchmarks and training-free consistency methods target identity, style, and
logical coherence across image sets~\cite{jia2025t2is,consistyle2025}.
These methods define consistency as a visual property.
They do not encode gameplay role ontologies, board-scale readability, or engine packaging, so a
visually consistent set can still be gameplay-inconsistent.
GAPGen adds gameplay consistency and runtime acceptance on top of this visual consistency.

\paragraph{Game sprites, tiles, and affordances.}
Sprite-sheet diffusion addresses character animation frames~\cite{spritesheet2024}, while
tile and affordance datasets connect low-resolution game art to semantic roles~\cite{gametilenet2024}.
We share the concern for gameplay semantics, but we target end-to-end generation of themed,
restylable, multi-family packs whose roles and stage progression stay consistent under a theme
swap, rather than retrieval or animation sheets alone.

\paragraph{Agentic creative refinement.}
Multi-agent creator and critic loops improve iterative image or 3D modeling quality through
planned critique~\cite{crea2025,planneractorcritic2026,scenecraft2024,shapecraft2025}.
Adjacent asset work further grounds generation in structure or physics~\cite{physx3d2025,neuralassets2024}.
We adopt the planner and critic pattern, but we specialize the critic to score gameplay
consistency, namely cutout integrity, fill-frame, no anthropomorphic faces, and progression
readability.

\section{Gameplay-Consistent Asset Pack Generation}
\label{sec:task}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Task definition}
\label{sec:task-def}

The input is a gameplay asset ontology that lists families, role classes, and stage progressions,
together with a style specification and an optional theme concept.
Given a target subset of assets, the task is to produce a pack of engine-ready sprites plus an
auditable trace of the plans, prompts, critiques, and scores that led to each one.

We judge a pack on three layers.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Production readiness.} Clean cutout, coverage, and illegal-outline or face checks.
  \item \textbf{Visual consistency.} Global style and intra-family cohesion, as in prior set
        generation.
  \item \textbf{Gameplay consistency.} Role and affordance recognition at board scale, and
        correct stage progression across a family.
\end{enumerate}
Production readiness and visual consistency can be checked automatically, while gameplay
consistency ultimately needs human judgment, so final acceptance stays with a human.

\section{GAPGen}
\label{sec:method}
% =============================================================================

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/GAPGen-system-overview.png}
  \caption{GAPGen pipeline: ontology planning, consistency generation under dual references, and
  verification with a revise-and-retry loop.}
  \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the three core generation stages of GAPGen.
The system runs ontology planning, consistency generation, and verification with a retry loop.
Section~\ref{sec:planning} covers planning, Section~\ref{sec:generation} covers consistency
generation with dual references, and Section~\ref{sec:critic} covers verification.

\subsection{Ontology planning}
\label{sec:planning}

A single global prompt under-specifies pack structure, so models freely reinterpret roles and
collapse neighboring damage states.
We instead read the game asset documents and the user prompt for theme and style, and expand them
into explicit plans.
A style planner turns the free-form style prompt into a locked rendering specification.
A theme planner maps each role to a themed object, for example a match element to an apple or a
row-clearer to a melon rocket, so that role and affordance stay fixed while appearance changes.
For multi-stage families, a stage planner produces an ordered stage plan and a reference chain
that starts from the intact anchor and moves toward the destroyed stage, with optional
family-style tokens for shared materials and ornaments.
Planning turns vague intent into inspectable artifacts such as role assignments, stage notes, and
style locks that later verification can cite, instead of burying all structure inside one opaque
image prompt.

\subsection{Consistency generation with dual references}
\label{sec:generation}

Independent per-asset sampling drifts in material language and stage identity, and flat
set-consistency methods miss the asymmetry between a same-family asset and the next depletion
step.
We therefore condition each prompt on the ontology, adding role class, preserve and avoid
constraints, and family cohesion rules.
Generation places the style anchor first.
A later asset may attach a style-anchor image to lock rendering language and a previous-stage
image to lock progression direction, and when restyling an existing pack the system may also
attach a prior-run sprite as a structural reference.
The two references separate two failure modes that a single reference entangles, namely style
drift and progression collapse, and the ontology makes functional requirements explicit before
any pixel is sampled.

\subsection{Verification}
\label{sec:critic}

Vision-language judges alone are weak at production defects such as halos, holes, and thin
silhouettes, while pixel heuristics alone cannot score directional affordance or theme fit.
We combine both and, crucially, we split the score into a visual-style part and a
gameplay-consistency part.
A deterministic programmatic check first runs chromakey cutout, bounding-box and opaque coverage,
and an outline or halo test.
A VLM critic then scores four axes, namely style and cohesion for visual consistency, and function
and progression for gameplay consistency, on a checkerboard composite that also exposes cutout
integrity.
Hard rules such as no facial features, no ink outlines, and fill-frame can force a retry
regardless of the soft scores.
When any axis falls below its threshold, the critic emits a revise instruction and the sprite is
regenerated with that feedback up to \(N\) iterations, otherwise the best-scoring candidate is
kept and marked \texttt{needs\_review}.
Splitting the two axes lets a sprite fail on gameplay consistency even when its visual style is
high, which is exactly the case that visual-only critics miss.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

We instantiate GAPGen for a Match-3 title covering fourteen asset families such as elements,
power-ups, crates, pools, chillers, and backgrounds.
Image generation uses \texttt{gemini-3.1-flash-image}, while critique and planners use
\texttt{gemini-3.5-flash}, and the programmatic check is deterministic.
Conditions B0 to B2 use \(N{=}1\) without the gameplay-consistency critic, while B3 uses
\(N{\le}3\) with the full critic.

\paragraph{Locked evaluation slice.}
For the controlled study we fix one rendering style, a 3D Disney cartoon look tuned for high
recognizability, one primary theme-swap concept \emph{Fruit}, and a minimal asset subset of five
match elements, directional power-ups, and the crate stage family from \texttt{Crt4} to
\texttt{Crt1}, for twelve assets total.
We additionally generate the same twelve-asset slice for \emph{Pet} and \emph{Ocean} under B0 to
B3 as extended automatic evidence and human-eval stimuli, with Steampunk omitted.

\subsection{Ablation study}
\label{sec:ablations}

To test the dual references and the gameplay-consistency critic on the Fruit slice, we
compare four conditions.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item[B0] Naive per-asset prompting, with no role ontology and no critic loop.
  \item[B1] Ontology-conditioned prompts only, with no style-anchor references and no critic.
  \item[B2] B1 plus style-anchor and previous-stage references, still with no critic.
  \item[B3] Full GAPGen, which is B2 plus the gameplay-consistency critic loop.
\end{description}
Each condition is run over three independent seeds because Gemini image generation is
non-deterministic, and we report mean~$\pm$~standard deviation over seeds.
We report pass rate under each condition's gates, the \texttt{needs\_review} rate,
iterations-to-accept, and, when the critic runs, mean style, function, cohesion, and progression
scores.

A higher pass rate is not better. Without the critic, B0 to B2 only check cutout and coverage-style
constraints and accept nearly every tile. B3 instead spends more iterations at \(1.95 \pm 0.34\)
and flags a variable \texttt{needs\_review} fraction at \(0.11 \pm 0.10\), so friction emerges
where soft production checks would have shipped every tile.
Re-scoring the shipped tiles with the same critic exposes what visual-only gates let through:
cohesion and crate progression stay low for B0 to B2 but rise to \(9.04\) and \(9.78\) for B3, and
a model-independent DINOv2 check agrees, with crate cohesion higher for B3 than B1 at \(0.81\)
against \(0.66\).

\subsection{Cross-model proxy evaluation}
\label{sec:human}

A full human study is left to future work. As proxies for readability, preference, and
consistency, images are generated with Gemini while the judge is GPT-4o, which avoids critic
self-circularity. We report three proxies mirroring the human protocol: role recognition at
\(70\)px as a forced 4-choice label over 8 assets, pairwise pack preference against B1, and DINOv2
intra-family cohesion on the crate family. A zh-Hant survey with the same tasks is prepared for
the human study.

\subsection{Qualitative observations}
\label{sec:qual}

Figure~\ref{fig:grids} shows full GAPGen (B3) packs across four themes for the locked asset subset.
One ontology drives every theme: elements stay color-separable, power-ups keep their directional or
explosive silhouette, and each crate depletes as the same object across four stages.
In development and across these themes we still observe failure modes that motivate the modules above.
Some stage pairs redraw different objects instead of depleting one object, some power-ups lose
their directionality after theme-swap, and under B3 the reasonableness check rejects cases such as
a warped power-up tip that programmatic-only gates would accept.
These observations are consistent with the proxy metrics in Table~\ref{tab:human}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

We recast structured game art generation as a gameplay consistency problem rather than a
visual-consistency one: a pack is done only when every asset also keeps its role, affordance, and
stage progression, not merely when its sprites share a style.
GAPGen enforces this through ontology planning, consistency generation under dual references, and
a critic that scores visual style and gameplay consistency on separate axes.
On our Fruit ablation, the critic trades a lower pass rate for retries and a variable
\texttt{needs\_review} fraction rather than maximizing accept rate, and cross-model proxies show
that the added stages improve readability and consistency.
A full human study on top of these proxies is our primary follow-up.
